How we think about compliance

A written policy is not the same thing as an operating control.

A governance document may describe the right process and still tell you little about what happens in production. We examine how requirements are assigned, what evidence is retained, how exceptions are handled and whether testing supports the claims being made about the system.

Work Example 01

ISO/IEC 42001 Gap assessment

Scope

AI-assisted customer-support workflow using a third-party language model, retrieval over approved knowledge content and human review before customer-facing messages are sent.

Assessment question

Can the organization show that AI governance responsibilities, risk decisions, supplier dependencies, change controls and oversight processes operate consistently across the system lifecycle?

Evidence examined

AI system register; defined roles; AI risk and impact assessment records; supplier documentation; change-approval records; evaluation plans; operating procedures; incident and exception logs; internal audit and management-review records.

Example assessment findings
Ref Area assessed Evidence issue Why it matters Corrective action / closure evidence
QMS-01 System inventory The workflow is listed as one application, but its model dependency, retrieval source and human approval point are not separately documented. Owners cannot reliably assess the consequences of a supplier-model change or a changed data source. Update the system inventory with components, dependencies, accountable owners, data flows and review triggers.
QMS-02 Risk and impact assessment Accuracy is assessed, but foreseeable impacts of unsupported answers, exposure of sensitive content and third-party model changes have no explicit acceptance criteria. A risk register may exist without guiding actual release decisions. Record impact scenarios, evaluation criteria, accountable approvers, treatment decisions and acceptance evidence.
QMS-03 Change management Releases require product-owner sign-off, but no documented re-evaluation thresholds exist for prompt, retrieval or model changes. A sign-off alone does not show that material changes were tested. Define change classes, required regression tests, approvals and retention of test results.
QMS-04 Evidence traceability Policies, test records and exception decisions are dispersed across documents and tickets. An assessor may be unable to follow a requirement through to operating evidence. Establish a requirements-to-control-to-evidence index with versioned records and accountable owners.
An excerpt from the evidence register
Requirement / question Responsible role Evidence to retain Pass condition
Who approves AI risk acceptance? Named risk owner Signed risk decisions, criteria and exception log Authority and decision criteria documented; material exceptions escalated
What happens when the underlying model changes? System owner Supplier change log, impact assessment, regression-test record Defined change classification and required tests completed before release
How is human oversight performed? Operations lead Reviewer instructions, sampled approval logs, escalation records Reviewers can intervene and exceptions are traceable
How are findings closed? Control owner Corrective-action plan, evidence of implementation, effectiveness check Closure is supported by re-tested or independently reviewed evidence
Deliverable structure
  1. Scope and applicability memo: AI systems, organizational boundaries, intended use, relevant dependencies and exclusions.
  2. Requirement-to-control matrix: Requirement, current process, accountable owner, supporting record, assessment result and gap.
  3. Prioritized corrective-action register: Remediation, responsible person, target state, closure evidence and status.
  4. Audit-readiness index: A navigable inventory of records to make review repeatable.

What the example demonstrates: A management-system assessment is not a checklist of policies. It tests whether responsibility, risk decisions, changes and records connect to the actual AI workflow.

Work Example 02

Algorithmic bias audit Selection-rate analysis

Scope

An automated screening tool used to rank applicants for further consideration and a set of 500 application records with a binary selected / not-selected outcome.

Audit question

Do selection outcomes differ by demographic category, and can the measures be reproduced from documented source data and a defined selection decision?

Metric definitions

Selection rate = number selected ÷ number evaluated in the category.

Impact ratio = category selection rate ÷ highest selection rate in the relevant comparison.

Analyze sex, race/ethnicity and intersectional groups as applicable; separately account for missing/unknown data.

Intersectional detail

The highest observed selection rate among the known intersectional groups is 60.0% for White men. The figures below use that rate as the denominator for impact ratios.

Category Evaluated Selected Selection rate Impact ratio
White men 110 66 60.0% 1.00
White women 90 44 48.9% 0.81
Black men 60 30 50.0% 0.83
Black women 60 18 30.0% 0.50
Hispanic / Latino men 50 20 40.0% 0.67
Hispanic / Latino women 50 20 40.0% 0.67
Asian men 25 9 36.0% 0.60
Asian women 45 17 37.8% 0.63

Ten records have unknown race/ethnicity (five men and five women), with one selection overall. These are retained in the population reconciliation and reported separately rather than silently discarded.

Findings that would require investigation
  • Subgroup variation: Several groups have materially lower observed selection rates than the highest-selection group; Black women have a 30.0% selection rate and a 0.50 impact ratio in the example.
  • Decision-point definition: Confirm that the binary outcome corresponds to the screening decision actually supported by the tool, not a later hiring decision influenced by unrelated stages.
  • Data completeness: Determine how demographic categories were collected, whether missing values are systematic and whether the data are suitable for the required audit.
  • Statistical interpretation: Inspect sample sizes and uncertainty. An impact ratio is a descriptive measure; it is not, by itself, proof of unlawful discrimination or of regulatory compliance.
  • Reproducibility: Retain the population definition, assessment period, data lineage, code or spreadsheet formulas, exclusions, model/version identification, and calculation checks.
Deliverable structure
  1. Methodology memorandum: Tool and use-case description; coverage and legal-scope analysis; audited population; decision definition; data source; category rules; exclusions; calculation method.
  2. Reproducible result tables: Counts, selection rates, impact ratios and intersectional analysis, including unknown categories.
  3. Limitations and follow-up questions: Data coverage, missingness, sample sizes, decision thresholds and potential confounders.
  4. Publication package: The information required for a public bias-audit summary, where the tool and its use fall within NYC Local Law 144, together with separate review of notice obligations.

What the example demonstrates: A defensible technical assessment shows the denominators, methods, calculations and limits of inference not just a pass/fail badge.

How an engagement runs

Four steps, with a record of each decision

01

Establish scope

Identify systems, intended uses, affected parties, organizational roles, applicable requirements and the evidence already available. Document exclusions rather than leaving them implicit.

02

Examine the controls

Map requirements to processes, owners and retained records. Distinguish documented policy from evidence that a process has operated.

Provide a prioritized register, evidence index and specific conditions for closing findings. The aim is to make subsequent review more efficient, not produce a report that no one uses.

Questions

Frequently asked

Does ISO/IEC 42001 certification establish EU AI Act compliance?

No. ISO/IEC 42001 addresses an AI management system; it does not automatically demonstrate conformity with every applicable AI Act obligation. EN 18286:2026 addresses the EU AI Act's Article 17 quality-management requirements and, as a cited harmonized standard, supports presumption of conformity only for the requirements it covers.

What is the difference between a gap assessment and an audit?

A gap assessment establishes the distance between current practices and a defined set of requirements. An audit evaluates evidence against specified criteria using a documented methodology. Some assignments combine both, but their purpose, independence conditions and conclusions should not be confused.

What do you need before a technical AI assessment can begin?

At minimum, we need to understand the system's intended use, its role in decisions, the version or configuration under review, the available test data and the relevant criteria. If those elements are missing, establishing them is usually the first task.
