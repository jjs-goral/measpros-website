The Regulatory Landscape

Two laws. Different mechanisms. The same underlying question.

New York and California have each taken a different approach to governing AI in employment but both reach the same core issue: whether automated tools produce outcomes that fall unequally across protected groups.

New York City

Local Law 144 Automated Employment Decision Tools

Any employer or employment agency that uses an AEDT to screen candidates for positions in NYC, or employees for promotion, must obtain an independent bias audit before use and repeat it annually. The law applies even when the tool is provided by a vendor the employer bears the duty.

The audit must calculate selection rates and impact ratios by sex, race/ethnicity, and intersectional categories. Demographic data cannot be inferred or imputed. Results are posted publicly on the employer's website for at least six months after the tool's last use.

Enforcement: DCWP, civil penalties up to $500 first violation / $500–$1,500 each subsequent violation per day of use
Effective: January 1, 2023 · Enforcement began July 5, 2023
Notice requirement: 10 business days advance notice to NYC-resident candidates before tool is used
California

CPPA ADMT Regulations Automated Decision-Making Technology

California's CCPA regulations extend privacy and fairness obligations to ADMT technology that processes personal information and uses computation to replace or substantially replace human decision-making. Employment decisions are explicitly within scope.

Businesses must provide pre-collection notice of ADMT use, conduct risk assessments documenting the system's logic and data types, and give consumers the right to opt out of significant decisions. A qualifying human appeal process can substitute for opt-out rights in hiring and promotion contexts.

Enforcement: California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA)
Effective: CCPA regulations in force January 1, 2026; ADMT compliance deadline January 1, 2027
Risk assessment: Required documentation of system logic, data types processed, and potential harms
NYC Local Law 144 Scope

What makes a tool an AEDT?

The law's definition is broader than many employers expect. It is not limited to sophisticated AI or machine learning a tool qualifies based on how it is used, not what it is built on.

Output determines the outcome

A tool is an AEDT when its output is used as the sole basis for a decision, is weighted more heavily than other criteria, or overrides a conclusion including a recruiter's judgment. The weight matters, not the label.

Vendor tools are your liability

If your ATS vendor, assessment platform, or resume-screening software qualifies as an AEDT and you use it to screen NYC candidates, the compliance obligation belongs to you not to the software company. Contracts do not transfer the duty.

Promotions are in scope

The law covers existing employees being screened for promotion, not only external candidates. An internal talent tool, a performance ranking system, or a promotion-eligibility filter may trigger the requirement.

Why Independence Is Not Optional

The auditor your vendor recommends may not qualify

The law is specific: the auditor cannot have an employment relationship with you or your AEDT vendor, cannot have played any role in developing or deploying the tool, and cannot have a material financial interest in either party. Many "audits" offered by AI platform vendors or by consultants with ongoing relationships with those vendors do not clear this bar.

The Regulatory Patchwork

NYC is the strictest but not the only

State and municipal AI hiring laws are multiplying. What satisfies NYC LL 144 today won't cover everything on the horizon. A documented audit program built around LL 144's requirements is a defensible foundation for the rules still taking shape.

Methods & Evidence

Work Example: Algorithmic bias audit

Selection-rate analysis for an automated screening tool. This example shows the denominators, methods, calculations and limits of inference that make an audit defensible.

Scope

An automated screening tool used to rank applicants for further consideration and a set of 500 application records with a binary selected / not-selected outcome.

Audit question

Do selection outcomes differ by demographic category, and can the measures be reproduced from documented source data and a defined selection decision?

Metric definitions

Selection rate = number selected ÷ number evaluated in the category.

Impact ratio = category selection rate ÷ highest selection rate in the relevant comparison.

Analyze sex, race/ethnicity and intersectional groups as applicable; separately account for missing/unknown data.

Findings that would require investigation
  • Subgroup variation: Several groups have materially lower observed selection rates than the highest-selection group; Black women have a 30.0% selection rate and a 0.50 impact ratio in the example.
  • Decision-point definition: Confirm that the binary outcome corresponds to the screening decision actually supported by the tool, not a later hiring decision influenced by unrelated stages.
  • Data completeness: Determine how demographic categories were collected, whether missing values are systematic and whether the data are suitable for the required audit.
  • Statistical interpretation: Inspect sample sizes and uncertainty. An impact ratio is a descriptive measure; it is not, by itself, proof of unlawful discrimination or of regulatory compliance.
  • Reproducibility: Retain the population definition, assessment period, data lineage, code or spreadsheet formulas, exclusions, model/version identification, and calculation checks.
Deliverable structure
  1. Methodology memorandum: Tool and use-case description; coverage and legal-scope analysis; audited population; decision definition; data source; category rules; exclusions; calculation method.
  2. Reproducible result tables: Counts, selection rates, impact ratios and intersectional analysis, including unknown categories.
  3. Limitations and follow-up questions: Data coverage, missingness, sample sizes, decision thresholds and potential confounders.
  4. Publication package: The information required for a public bias-audit summary, where the tool and its use fall within NYC Local Law 144, together with separate review of notice obligations.

What the example demonstrates: A defensible technical assessment shows the denominators, methods, calculations and limits of inference not just a pass/fail badge.
